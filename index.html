The Pine Walrus

Odobenus pinicola

North America’s Forgotten Forest Giant

The pine walrus (Odobenus pinicola) is a rarely observed terrestrial relative of the common walrus, believed to inhabit dense coniferous forests and isolated wetlands across northern North America. Unlike its marine cousins, the pine walrus is adapted to life on land, using its shortened tusks to strip bark, uncover roots, and move fallen branches while foraging. Its thick, dark-brown hide is often coated with pine resin, soil, and evergreen needles, providing remarkably effective camouflage on the forest floor. Most reported sightings occur near remote lakes and bogs shortly before dawn.

The name “pine walrus” is thought to have originated with nineteenth-century timber crews who reported deep, rhythmic grunts from within old-growth forests. Early accounts described a large, tusked animal resting beneath fallen trees and carrying the strong scent of fresh-cut pine. Although initially dismissed as logging-camp folklore, similar descriptions appeared independently across several northern settlements, leading naturalists to adopt the name in regional field records. No complete specimen has ever been formally cataloged, and the pine walrus remains one of North America’s least understood large mammals.
